The Physicality of an AI Model

Prepared by Saadiq Industries
Sheets 05
Revision A

You said a piece of technology is code, stored, and run against data. That is exactly right, and it is why AI does not fit.

In a conventional system the code is the asset. Somebody wrote it, you can read it, you can diff it, you own it, and it operates on data that arrives from outside.

An AI model turns that around. The data is the code. The model is a file of numbers that behaves like a program, and no human wrote a single one of those numbers. The program that runs the file is free and boring. Once you see that, the questions about where it lives, who owns it, and what happens to your prompts all answer themselves.

Sheet 01

What the thing physically is

A model is a file. Not a program, not a database, not a service. A directory of files containing several hundred billion numbers, each stored in about two bytes, adding up to somewhere in the hundreds of gigabytes. There is no logic inside it. No formulas, no field names, no comments, nothing a person can read.

The code that runs the file is a few thousand lines of arithmetic, and you can download it for nothing. The code is the commodity. The numbers are the product.

Sheet 02

Where the thing physically is

At rest the file sits in storage, on disks, in a named region. To answer a single question, the whole thing has to be loaded into the memory of graphics processors, and a frontier model does not fit on one chip. It gets split across eight of them inside one server. So at the moment you ask it something, the model exists as electric charge in memory, on cards, in a chassis, in a rack, in a building, in a state, under a specific contract.

Sheet 03

What happens when you use it

This is the plate that matters most, because it settles the question everybody is quietly asking. When you paste a board deck into Copilot, does the model now know your board deck?

Mechanically, no. The weights are frozen. During a request they are read, never written. Using the model does not change the model. What actually happens is that the vendor writes your text to a log, that log sits on a disk for however many days your contract permits, and a future training run might draw on it if your contract permits that too.

The leak vector is the log, not the model. Which means the thing to govern is the retention term, not the algorithm.

The corollary

The model also has no memory. What Copilot calls memory is a separate database of notes that gets pasted back into the prompt every single time, and the whole conversation is re-sent on every turn. It is not remembering. It is re-reading. That is also why the token bill looks the way it does.

Sheet 04

Four things, four addresses

People say "the AI has our data" because they have collapsed four separate objects into one. They sit in four different places, under four different owners, for four different lengths of time. Name them separately and the governance work becomes obvious.

Where each object physically sits, who controls it, and how long it persists
Object Where it physically sits Who controls it How long it persists Yours
01THE WEIGHTS Storage at the lab, and in GPU memory whenever a request is running The lab. Or you, if the weights are open and self-hosted. Frozen until a new training run produces a new file Rented
02YOUR PROMPT & THE RESPONSE The vendor's log store. Transiently, a cache in GPU memory during the request. The vendor, bounded by your contract Zero to thirty-plus days. Frontier tiers often carry a retention floor. Exposed
03THE TRAINING DATA The lab's data lake. Petabytes. Not inside the model. The lab Retained by the lab. The model is a lossy compression of it, not a copy. Not yours
04YOUR CORPUS, RETRIEVAL INDEX, PROMPTS, EVALS Your own storage. The model only ever sees this as text inside a prompt. You As long as you keep it Owned

Row 03 is the one that surprises people. Petabytes go in and a few hundred gigabytes come out, so the training data cannot be in there. What survives is statistical regularity, not text. The exception, and it is the hinge of every copyright case now in court, is that material repeated often enough in training can sometimes be reconstructed close to verbatim.

Sheet 05

Ownership schedule

Ownership attaches to objects. There are four of them here, and they have four different answers, which is why "do we own the AI" has no answer as asked. This is also the schedule to put in front of any vendor, because silence in a contract defaults every line to whoever physically holds the thing.

What protects each object, and who ends up owning it
Object What legally protects it Who ends up owning it
01THE WEIGHTS Not copyright, most likely. Copyright protects human creative expression, and no human authored these numbers. The labs rely on trade secret, contract, and simply never shipping the file. Whoever holds the file. There is no lock on it. The moat is possession, not law, which is why a leaked weights file is a total loss and why open-weight releases live or die by their license terms.
02THE TRAINING DATA Fair use, contested, and being decided case by case. The pattern so far is that courts have looked far harder at how the corpus was acquired than at the act of training on it. The lab, along with the liability. Provenance is what gets litigated. Learning from lawfully obtained material has fared better than building the library in the first place.
03THE OUTPUTS Nothing, as to the machine's contribution. Under US law purely machine-generated expression carries no copyright. Add real human selection and arrangement and the human part is protectable. The machine's part never is. Nobody. Vendors assign you their rights in outputs, but they cannot assign a copyright that does not exist. What you are actually buying is a promise plus an indemnity. Read its carve-outs. They all require you to use the vendor's guardrails.
04DERIVED WEIGHTS (fine-tunes) The base model's license, plus whatever your vendor agreement says. Whoever holds the file. Fine-tune on a hosted service and you do not get a file, you get an endpoint. You pay to create a model you can only rent, and you cannot take it anywhere.
05THE CORPUS, THE RETRIEVAL INDEX, THE PROMPT LIBRARY, THE EVAL SUITE, THE GOLDEN DATASETS Trade secret, and your own contract language. These are the only objects on this sheet you can protect the ordinary way. You, if and only if the agreement names each one. This is the row to negotiate. Everything above it is somebody else's.

Directional, not advice  ·  Verify before citing
Legal posture moves quarterly. Confirm the current state of the case law and the vendor's indemnity terms before any of this is repeated in a board setting.

The engine is a frozen file you rent and can never really own. Everything you can own is the car.

The corpus, the retrieval layer, the prompt library, the eval suite. That is the ownership map, and it turns out to be the same map as the value map. Most people arrive at that from the moat argument. The physicality gets you there from the opposite direction.

So the answer to the original question. Your data does not go into the model, it goes into a log. The model is not in the cloud, it is in a building. It is not code that runs against data, it is data that runs as code. And nobody owns it by authorship, only by holding on to the file.
