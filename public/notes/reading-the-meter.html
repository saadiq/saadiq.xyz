READING 01

The three dials

Input is what you send. That means the question, the documents, the conversation so far, and the instructions the software wraps around all of it. Output is what comes back. Both bill per word, at different rates.

The third dial does not appear anywhere in the product. A reasoning model writes to itself before it writes to you. That private writing bills as output and never reaches you. You pay for it and never read it.

Those words are the work. Turn the dial down on the same weights and the model gets worse. Given a competition‑math benchmark, gpt‑oss‑120b scores 56% at low effort and 96% at high. The thinking is what buys the second number, and on a hard problem it is the best money on this page.

It also runs when the question does not need it. Asked to multiply two three‑digit numbers, one model produced 3,657 words of thinking to deliver a 214‑word answer, and the thinking bought nothing. Across 77 models, turning reasoning on multiplies words by about 8x. The dial is on by default, and it does not know what you asked.

Two things compound this. Conversation history re‑bills as input on every turn, so turn ten pays again for turns one through nine. And an agent uses 5x to 30x the words of a chat turn, at an input‑to‑output ratio closer to 80:1 than the 8:1 of a conversation.

READING 02

Why a hard question costs more

The picture most people arrive at is a cross‑section of the earth. One model reaches 15 layers down, a better one reaches 25, a cheap one works the topsoil. The diamond is buried, so the deeper model finds it, and the digging shows up on the bill.

The economics in that picture are right. The mechanism is wrong, and the difference changes what you buy.

A model's layers are fixed. Every word it handles passes through all of them. The word “the” costs the same trip as the hardest word in a proof about relativity. A model cannot dig deeper on a hard question.

What it does instead is write more. Each word it writes to itself is another trip through the full stack, and what it has already written tells it what to write next. A hard answer costs more because the model takes thousands of identical passes, not because any pass goes deeper.

The model you would call topsoil

DeepSeek V4‑Pro has 1.6 trillion parameters. Kimi K2.6 has 1 trillion. OpenAI, Anthropic and Google do not publish theirs. These models are cheap because only 32B to 49B parameters activate for any given word, not because they are small.

READING 03

Cost per answer

Thinking and capability substitute for each other. A weaker model deliberates longer to reach an answer a stronger one reaches quickly, and it does not fully arrive.

Claude Sonnet 5 spends 68,736 output words per benchmark task and scores 53 on the composite intelligence index. GPT‑5.6 Sol spends 15,346 and scores 59. Sonnet thinks 4.5 times as long and lands 6 points lower.

The same trade runs across time. Scoring 27% on FrontierMath took one model 43 million words of thinking in April 2025. Eight months later a newer model reached the same 27% on 5 million, at its cheapest effort setting.

Multiply words per task by price per word, and the ranking changes.

The ordering changes twice

GPT‑5.6 Sol charges 3x Sonnet 5 per word and costs 1.5x less per task.
It charges 1.2x Opus 4.8 per word and costs 2.2x less per task.

Comparing vendors on dollars per million tokens produces the wrong answer. The number that produces the right one is not on the price sheet.

Effort has diminishing returns on top of that. Pushing a frontier model from medium to maximum costs 4x to 5x the words for a gain of 2 to 5 points. Fable 5 costs 3.6x what Sol costs per task and scores 1 point higher.

So the money sits in two decisions. Which model answers the question, and how hard you let it think about it. Both are set per request, and most organizations set neither.

READING 04

What the cheap models give up

The ladder runs 950x from top to bottom for the same English sentence.

On five of six measures the open models sit close to the frontier. On the sixth they do not.

Calibration

DeepSeek V4‑Pro scores 88.8 on graduate science against Opus 4.8's 92.0, and 87.5 on broad knowledge, which is parity. Asked a question it cannot answer, it produces an answer anyway 94% of the time. The frontier models say they do not know.

For call summaries, document classification and boilerplate, that costs nothing. For a question about a Medicare rule, a confident wrong answer carries regulatory risk, and no benchmark a vendor will show you measures it.

Before anyone shows you a benchmark

Of roughly 100 models on the main coding leaderboard, one score was checked by anyone other than the lab that produced the model.

And the open‑weights frontier is now entirely Chinese. Meta shipped its latest model closed in April 2026, the first time in three years. Llama 4 Maverick, still its open flagship, scores 18 on the composite index. The next lowest model on this page scores 40.

READING 05

Running the models yourself

The weights are public, so you can run them on your own hardware and stop paying per word. You pay for the machine instead, and the machine bills 24 hours a day whether or not anyone is asking it anything.

GLM 5.2 needs 893GB. One Azure node holds 1,128GB across 8 accelerators. It does not fit on the previous generation's 640GB.

That node costs $84.80 an hour on demand in West US 2, or $37.31 on a three‑year reservation, which is $27,244 a month before anyone types anything. Tuned, it produces 4,176 to 6,311 output words a second.

Against Azure's own managed GLM endpoint, one node pays for itself above 34% to 51% utilization. Across 20,000 employees that is about 27 answers per person per working day, which an organization serious about AI would clear.

Hardware against payroll

The node costs $326,923 a year. The team that keeps a frontier mixture‑of‑experts model serving in production, tuning expert parallelism, swapping model versions without dropping traffic, and carrying the pager, costs $500,000 to $1,500,000.

One spare node for failover moves break‑even utilization to 68%, or past 100%, depending on which throughput figure holds.

The compliance premium

DeepSeek charges $0.87 per million output words for its own model. Microsoft charges $3.83 to serve the identical file. Nothing about the model changed. The 4.4x difference is the compliance premium, and that is the choice on the table.

The premium buys a business associate agreement, a tenant the data never leaves, and a vendor who carries the pager.

To use the best American model on protected health information you must sign that agreement, and Anthropic's requires a 30‑day minimum retention window. Zero retention is not compatible with it. The rule you reach for to protect the data is the rule that forces the log to exist.

A self‑hosted model has no vendor, no log and no retention term. No US health plan has published doing this. Two hospital systems have, one of them peer‑reviewed, running an open Chinese model on their own machines inside their own network and wired into their medical records.

Azure already sells these weights managed inside your own tenant. GLM 5.2 at $4.84 a million words out, DeepSeek V4‑Pro at $3.83, Kimi K2.6 at $4.00. Microsoft's agreement covers them and the data stays in the tenant.

READING 06

Where the cost is going

The price of a fixed capability is falling 5x to 10x a year. Scoring 27% on FrontierMath took one model 43 million output words in April 2025. Eight months later another model scored the same 27% on 5 million, at its cheapest setting.

The cost of running frontier models is rising 3x to 18x a year. Claude Opus fell from $15 and $75 per million to $5 and $25 across its life. Fable 5 then replaced it at the top at $10 and $50.

Reasoning models are getting more verbose at about 5x a year, against 2.2x for models that do not reason. The frontier gets expensive because answers get longer, and you are billed for the part you never see.

Meanwhile an agent uses 5x to 30x the words of a chat turn. Uber spent its annual budget for coding assistants in four months and capped its engineers at $1,500 a month each.

For the board

Our cost per answer will fall every year. Our AI bill will rise every year. Both are true, and where we land on a 900x spread is set by routing discipline, not by vendor pricing.

The flat rate already ended

Anthropic's enterprise plan is $20 a seat for access and nothing else. Every word your people type bills at metered rates on top of it, from the first one. GitHub moved its entire Copilot business onto metered credits on 1 June 2026, and reported monthly bills went from $29 to around $750. Microsoft began billing agent work by consumption on 1 July 2026, on top of the flat $30 seat.

“GitHub has absorbed much of the escalating inference cost. The current model is no longer sustainable. A quick chat question and a multi‑hour autonomous coding session can cost the user the same amount.”

Mario Rodriguez · Chief Product Officer, GitHub · April 2026

Two vendors still sell a flat seat with no meter behind it. Both are absorbing the difference. Neither has committed to continuing. An AI budget built from headcount times a seat price is a forecast of last year.

Routing

Most executives already let a model gather and compress information, and never let it make the decision at the end. That is routing by what a wrong answer costs, with the line drawn between the machine and themselves. The same line, drawn between models, is the routing table.

Open weights for the wide base of work where a mistake is free. The best‑calibrated model available for the narrow top where it is not. Most of what an organization does all day sits in the base, and few companies employ anyone whose job is to draw the line.

LIMITS

What this leaves out

Utilization. The 40% figure driving the self‑hosting arithmetic is an industry rule of thumb. No vendor document supports it, and it sits directly on the crossover, so it carries more weight than it should.

Throughput. Nobody publishes an aggregate benchmark for GLM 5.2 or DeepSeek V4‑Pro. The 4,176 to 6,311 range is measured on DeepSeek‑R1, an architectural twin, on the same hardware. An earlier draft of this document used vendor cookbook figures measured at low concurrency and overstated self‑hosting cost by a factor of four to six.

Benchmarks. Almost every open‑weights score here is reported by the lab that made the model. Anthropic's headline coding number used Anthropic's own scaffolding. FrontierMath carried errors in 42% of its original problems, corrected a year later.

Input. The cost‑per‑task figures count output only. Input is cheaper and can be cached at roughly 90% off, and conversation history re‑bills every turn. Neither is modelled here.

Licenses. GLM 5.2 is plain MIT. MiniMax M3 requires a negotiated license above $20M revenue. Llama 4 carries a 700 million user clause and excludes EU entities. Read the license, not the download button.

Security. A self‑hosted model cannot send data anywhere if you control its network egress. It can still behave badly on the topics its makers cared about. That is an evaluation problem, and this document does not cover it.

Legal. Nothing here is advice on copyright, procurement or compliance. The retention and BAA terms cited are current as of 9 July 2026, and Anthropic restructured its agreement twice in the preceding eight months.
